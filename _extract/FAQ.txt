PART 1 — THE FOUR MEASUREMENTS
What exactly is “accuracy”, and how do I compute it?
Of the items you tested, the fraction the system got right. Nothing more sophisticated is required. Both approaches must be
scored on the same test items, or the two numbers cannot be compared.
accuracy = sum(1 for p, t in zip(predictions, truth) if p == t) / len(truth)
 
# always print this next to it:
from collections import Counter
baseline = Counter(truth).most_common(1)[0][1] / len(truth)
# baseline = the score you get by ALWAYS guessing the most common category,
# while knowing nothing. An accuracy that cannot beat it is not a result.
If your categories are lopsided — 80% of your examples in one bucket — an 80% accuracy means your system has learned
nothing. This is the single most common way a submission looks good and says nothing. Report the baseline every time.

How do I measure latency properly?
Wall-clock time for one prediction. Measure it per item and report both the median and the slowest few — the mean alone
hides the one call that took four seconds, and in a real system it is the slow tail that users complain about.

You keep saying “at what scale would your answer change?” — what does scale mean here?
Scale, for this assignment, is one number: how many items your system would have to handle over its working life. Not how
big your company is. Not how much data exists in the world. How many times this specific task would run.
Pick the unit that fits your domain and say what it is — support tickets per month, patients per clinic per day, match reports per
season, products in the catalogue. Then find your crossover: the volume at which the cheaper option stops being the cheaper
option. Put a value on your own time; any defensible rate works, as long as you state it.
YOUR_HOURLY_RATE = 25       # US$/hour — state whatever you use, and why
LABELLING_HOURS  = 3.5      # what it actually took you, scaled to a real dataset
 
one_off_cost_of_owning = LABELLING_HOURS * YOUR_HOURLY_RATE
crossover_items        = one_off_cost_of_owning / cost_per_item
 
print(f'below {crossover_items:,.0f} items the API is cheaper overall;'
      f' above it, the classifier is')
Then say the sentence out loud in your writeup: “At my domain's realistic volume of about N items a year, the crossover sits at
M, so I would deploy X — and I would revisit that if volume rose past M, or if the price of the model fell by half.” That
sentence is the assignment.
Three things scale changes, not one. Cost is the obvious one. It is not the only one, and a strong submission names at least
two:
  Unit economics — the crossover above.
 Throughput and rate limits — 400 ms per item is invisible at a hundred items and is eleven hours at a hundred thousand.
Providers also cap calls per minute.
 Consequence of error — a 3% error rate is three wrong answers at a hundred items and thirty thousand at a million. The
accuracy did not change; what it means did.

Why retrieval and not the model? Because retrieval is the part you built. The model is rented and interchangeable; your
chunk size, your overlap, your top-k and your documents are the system. When a retrieval-augmented system disappoints in
industry, it is almost never the model — it is that the right passage never reached it.
A weak answer says “it got the question wrong.” A strong one says “the chunk containing the answer ranked fourth at 0.31,
while an unrelated chunk scored 0.44 because both contain the word water; my chunks are 45 words, so the distinguishing
context was split into the neighbouring chunk.” Same failure. A band apart.
How do I find an edge on purpose, rather than hoping one turns up?
Attack it in four specific ways — the starter notebook has all four wired up:
  Vocabulary mismatch — ask using a word that never appears in your documents but means the same thing.
 Two documents needed — ask something that requires combining facts from two places. Retrieval fetches the most
similar chunks, which is not the same as the set that answers the question.
 Distractor — ask about a topic several documents touch, where only one is relevant.

PART 3 — WHAT A “HARNESS” IS
What is a “harness”? Is it the prompt? The model?
Neither. The harness is the code around your prompt that runs it against every test case automatically and scores each
one — so that re-running the whole set after a change costs you one click instead of ten minutes of reading.
It has exactly three parts:
 (1) The cases — your ten inputs, each with what a correct answer must look like.
(2) The runner — a loop that sends every case through the prompt and collects the outputs.
(3) The scorer — the checks that turn each output into pass or fail, at both levels.
def run(prompt_fn, cases):          # <- this loop IS the harness
    rows = []
    for c in cases:
        output = generate(prompt_fn(c.text))
        rows.append({
            'case': c,
            'l1':   all(ok for _, ok in l1_checks(output, c)),   # machine checks
            'l2':   judge(c, output),                            # judgement check
        })
    pass_l1 = sum(r['l1'] for r in rows) / len(rows)
    pass_l2 = sum(r['l2'] for r in rows) / len(rows)
    return rows, pass_l1, pass_l2
Why it matters more than the prompt. Without a harness you change a prompt, read three outputs, decide it looks better,
and move on — and you will be wrong roughly half the time, because you did not re-check the seven cases that were already
passing. The harness is what makes prompting an experiment rather than an opinion. It is the same idea as a test suite in
software: the value is not in running it once, it is in running it after every change.
The harness is also the deliverable. The brief asks for a runnable notebook — a marker should be able to re-run your cells and
watch your numbers reappear.

“Explain every block of code” — how much do I really need to know?
Block, not line. For each cell or coherent chunk of your notebook you should be able to say what it does and why it is there —
what goes in, what comes out, and what would break if you deleted it. You are not being tested on syntax. Nobody will ask
you to recite what a list comprehension is or why there is a colon at the end of a line. What matters is that you know what each
piece of your build is for.
So: “this cell splits my examples into a training set and a test set, so the classifier is scored on items it has never seen” is
exactly the right level. “This cell calls train_test_split with stratify” is not required, and is not worth extra.
What counts toward the 1,200 words?
Your combined writeup only — roughly 400 words per part. Code, comments, notebook output, tables, the self-appraisal
cover sheet and any appendix do not count. The cap exists to stop you padding; if you are at 700 words and have said
everything, you are finished.

A cell in the starter notebook does not work. Is it broken?
Possibly, and that is worth knowing about. These notebooks were written before any of you ran them, against library versions,
model names and provider behaviour that all change without notice. Assume they will need occasional repair rather than
assuming they are infallible.
What to do, in order:
 (1) Read the error. The last line of a Python traceback usually names the problem in plain words — a missing package, a
model your key cannot reach, a network timeout.
(2) Try to fix it yourself. Debugging is not a detour from this assignment; it is a large part of what building anything
actually consists of, and working out why something broke is Technical Execution and Reasoning at the same time.
(3) Report it — in class or by email — with the cell it happened in, the full error text, and what you already tried. I will fix
the shared copy and tell the cohort, so nobody behind you loses the same hour.
Reporting a problem costs you nothing and is not an admission that you are struggling. A starter notebook is shared
infrastructure — the first person to hit a bug and say so saves everyone who comes after them. That is exactly how a working
team behaves, and it is a better instinct to leave this course with than a perfect score.

How do I choose which model to use?
Deliberately — and this is not a side issue. Choosing a model against a budget is one of the skills this course exists to teach,
and Part 1 asks you to measure exactly the thing that should drive the choice.
  Start on the cheap default. The starter notebooks ship with a small, inexpensive model. Begin there every time, and make
it prove itself inadequate before you spend more.
 Only move up on evidence. Your accuracy figure in Part 1 and your pass rates in Part 3 are the evidence. “The bigger
model felt better” is not — noticing that is the entire point of building an eval harness.
 Know the spread. On openrouter.ai/models the price range across models is not a few percent; it is orders of
magnitude. Input and output are priced separately, and output usually costs several times more than input.
 Open-weight models are often the sweet spot. Qwen, DeepSeek and Llama variants cost a fraction of frontier prices and
are strong at exactly the narrow, structured tasks this assignment asks for.
 Avoid reasoning models here. They bill you for thinking tokens you never see, which is poor value on a one-word
classification or a small JSON extraction.
 Compare on cost per item, not cost per token — and remember latency is one of your four measurements, so a
cheap-but-slow model is a real trade-off, not a free win.
If you do try a second model, report both. A paragraph showing that the expensive model bought you two percentage points
for eight times the cost is worth more marks than a single number from whichever model you happened to pick.
How do I check how much credit I have left?
OpenRouter will tell you. From a terminal:
curl https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/key \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer YOUR_OPENROUTER_API_KEY"
It replies with JSON that looks like this:
{
  "data": {
    "label": "My Key Name",
    "usage": 0.45,          // total spent on this key, in US$
    "limit": 10.00,         // your spending cap (null if none is set)
    "is_free_tier": false,
    "rate_limit": { "requests": 200, "interval": "10s" }
  }
}
What you have left is limit minus usage — $9.55 in that example. Two other fields are worth knowing: rate_limit tells
you how many requests you may make in a given window, which is what you have hit when a cell fails and then works a
minute later; and is_free_tier should be false on your key.
No terminal? Run it from the notebook instead. Each starter notebook already has a credit_check() cell just after the
setup — run it whenever you like. Checking your balance is itself free; this endpoint is not billed.

What if I run out of API credit?
Credit will not be topped up. The allocation on your key is fixed and is sized to cover A1, A2 and the End-of-Course Project

Running out is therefore a signal, not an accident. Doing this assignment as designed costs a small fraction of your allocation,
