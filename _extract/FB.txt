Rubric 1 (four criteria)
Read this first if you have never written code. Two-thirds of this cohort arrived without a technical background — you are the
majority, not the exception. This assignment is built for you. Every part comes with a starter notebook where the scaffolding is
already written; your job is to make choices, run things, and explain what happened.
Half the marks are for thinking, not coding. Technical Execution is 30%. Conceptual Understanding (25%) and Reasoning &
Justification (25%) together are 50%. A modest build that you can explain clearly and justify honestly will outscore an elaborate
one you cannot account for.
WHAT THIS IS FOR
In Class 1 you discovered that you cannot predict what AI is good at by asking how hard a task looks — the capability frontier
is jagged, and the only reliable way to find its edge is to test. A1 is that argument turned into work. Rather than reasoning
about which approach is better, you will build competing approaches, measure them, and defend a choice with evidence.
PICK YOUR DOMAIN FIRST
All three parts use one domain of your choosing. Pick something you genuinely know — you will be judged partly on
whether you can tell a sensible answer from a nonsense one, and that requires real knowledge.
It does not need to be a business context. Good choices include your undergraduate subject, a hobby you have practised for
years, a part-time job, a city or country you know well, a sport, a game, a language, a cuisine, a body of fiction. Choose the
thing you could talk about for an hour without preparation.
Scope check. If your domain is “healthcare” it is too broad. “Triage notes in an emergency department” is workable. If it is “my
grandmother's recipes” it may be too small to find 10–30 documents for Part 2 — though it is a perfectly good choice for Parts 1
and 3. Ask if you are unsure. Asking early is not a weakness.
WHAT YOU SUBMIT — THREE PARTS AND A SELF-APPRAISAL
Part 1 · Choose the right tool (Week 1–2 material)
Take a classification task in your domain — sorting things into two or more categories. Build it twice.
 (a) A scikit-learn classifier trained on labelled examples.
(b) A foundation-model API call with a prompt, and no training at all.
Measure both on accuracy, cost, latency, and effort. Then answer the question that matters: which would you actually deploy
— and at what scale would your answer change?
Deliverable: one notebook, a comparison table, and roughly 400 words.
Part 2 · Build a minimal retrieval system (Week 2 material)
Assemble a small collection of documents from your domain — 10 to 30 is enough. Build a system that retrieves relevant
passages and uses them to answer questions.
  Show it answering five questions it could not possibly answer without your documents.
 Then break it. Find a question where retrieval fails or returns the wrong passage, and explain why it failed.

Part 3 · Prompt and evaluate (Week 2–3 material)
Choose a task in your domain that a model could plausibly do. Write a prompt for it. Then build a ten-case evaluation set —
ten inputs, each with the output you would accept as correct.
This part does not use Part 2. You are evaluating a plain prompt sent straight to the model — no retrieval, no documents,
no RAG. In Class 2 you saw the evaluation harness applied to the retrieval build and in Class 3 to prompting; the technique is
the same either way, and here you point it at a direct prompt. If you would rather evaluate your Part 2 retrieval system instead,
you may — but it earns no extra marks, and the simpler route is the safer one.
  Grade every case at two levels, as demonstrated in Classes 2 and 3. Level 1 (L1) — automatic assertions: checks a
machine can run on its own (the format is valid, the required fields are present, a value falls in range, a label matches).
Every case gets these. Level 2 (L2) — judgement: at least one check that no assertion can make — an LLM-as-judge (a
second model scoring the output against your criteria) or your own human rating — for whether the answer is actually right
and actually usable.
 Run the prompt against all ten. Report the pass rate at each level.
 Change the prompt once. Re-run all ten. Report what moved at L1 and at L2, and say whether you expected it.
Keep the harness runnable — the deliverable is a working evaluation harness, not an essay about evaluation. If L1 passes and
L2 fails, that gap is the most interesting thing in your submission: say what it tells you.
Deliverable: one notebook containing the runnable harness, your eval set, both result tables (L1 and L2, before and after),
and roughly 400 words.
Self-appraisal (required, not graded)
A short cover sheet, submitted with your work. It is not marked, but a submission without it is incomplete.
  Rate yourself against each of the four criteria (Excellent / Proficient / Developing / Limited).
 Two or three sentences on Criterion 3 — Reasoning & Justification: what trade-offs did you weigh, and what would you
fix with more time?
 And one question specific to this course: where did an AI assistant genuinely help you, and where did it confidently
mislead you? You are studying the jagged frontier. You will have just walked across it.

USING AI TO BUILD AI
You may use AI assistants for this assignment. You would be a strange student of this subject if you did not. Three
conditions:
 (1) You must be able to explain every block of code you submit — what it does, and why it is there. Block, not line: you
are not being tested on syntax, only on whether you know what each piece of your build is for.
(2) Verify the measurements yourself. An assistant will happily invent a plausible accuracy figure. Your marks depend on
your numbers being real.
(3) Report the experience in your self-appraisal — where it helped, where it misled you.
All individually-submitted work must be your own, with authorship clearly attributed. NTU's academic integrity policy applies
in full.

A NOTE ON AMBITION
The strongest submissions in an assignment like this are rarely the most technically elaborate. They are the ones where a student
picked a domain they truly understood, built something modest, tested it honestly, found where it broke, and explained all of that
clearly. Build something small that you understand completely.
